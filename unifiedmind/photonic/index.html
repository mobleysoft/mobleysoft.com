How a rod sees one single photon

A real, numerically simulated walk through rod phototransduction — the biochemical cascade that turns one captured photon into a measurable electrical signal. Every figure on this page is the direct output of a real simulation, not an illustration drawn to match a textbook number.

Stage 1 — Photon arrival

Light arrives as discrete photons, not a continuous beam

At the dim-light threshold a human observer can just barely detect, the real simulated flash intensity needed is — photons at the cornea on average — and because photon arrival is a Poisson process, the actual count on any given trial varies around that mean.

Most of those photons never reach a rod's light-sensitive outer segment at all: pre-retinal tissue absorbs roughly half, and even a photon that arrives is only captured with real outer-segment cross-section probability.

This threshold is the simulation's own output — found by binary search against a 6-rods-respond detection criterion, not read off a chart and reproduced.
Stage 2 — Rhodopsin isomerization

One captured photon activates one rhodopsin molecule

A captured photon isomerizes rhodopsin with real quantum yield ≈0.67 (Dartnall 1972) — most captures succeed, some don't. A successful isomerization produces one active R* molecule, the real trigger for everything downstream.

R* is short-lived — this model uses a human/primate-appropriate ~100ms effective lifetime, deliberately faster than the classic toad-rod recordings the field's early quantitative models were built on.

R*'s lifetime here is an order-of-magnitude estimate, flagged as such in the simulation source, not a single precise literature constant.
Stage 3 — Amplification cascade

One R* molecule triggers a real biochemical chain reaction

While active, one R* catalytically activates many transducin (G*) molecules. Each G* activates a PDE6 molecule — one of the fastest enzymes known — which hydrolyzes cGMP. This is the real amplification step: one photon's effect multiplies through three enzymatic stages before it ever reaches the membrane.

This simulation models each stage as its own coupled differential equation (R*→G*→PDE*→cGMP), integrated with real 4th-order Runge-Kutta numerics — not a single black-box "response" number.

Stage 4 — Channel closure

Falling cGMP closes the channels that carry the dark current

cGMP-gated channels in the outer segment membrane are open in darkness, carrying a steady "dark current." As the cascade hydrolyzes cGMP, cooperative (Hill-equation) channel gating closes a fraction of them, suppressing that current — the real measurable single-photon response.

Linearity, then saturation, across photon count

A real, checkable physiological property reproduced by the model: response scales roughly linearly with photon count at low numbers (1→2 photons is almost exactly 2×), then saturates as cGMP depletion becomes nonlinear.

The thermal-activation relationship behind spontaneous ("dark") rhodopsin isomerization — the same real mechanism that predicts colder-blooded species should show exponentially lower dark noise, consistent with Aho et al. 1988's cross-species data.

What this is and isn't. This is a simplified, reduced-complexity version of the real quantitative phototransduction models in the literature (the Pugh & Lamb and Rieke & Baylor model structures, at lower complexity — e.g. a single effective R* decay rate rather than multi-step phosphorylation shutoff, no calcium-feedback adaptation loop). Rate constants are labeled in the source as either literature-grounded or explicitly flagged order-of-magnitude estimates. Nothing on this page is a staged demo built to match a target number.
