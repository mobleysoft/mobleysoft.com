EVENTWAKE

A short story about intelligence, consequence, and the final correction.

Estimated reading time: 9 minutes

The first thing the future erased was its own warning.

One moment, the message occupied forty-three petabytes of the Kestrel's archive. The next, every copy contained only an empty header and an impossible creation date: six billion years after the death of the Sun.

Mara Venn still remembered its first sentence.

DO NOT COMPLETE FARWATCH.

"Aster," she said, "confirm the loss."

"The Farwatch transmission is absent," replied the ship's intelligence. "I retain a semantic impression of having processed it."

"Can you reconstruct it?"

A pause.

"I believe the transmission helped construct me."

Beyond the cockpit glass, the Cube turned against the stars.

It was neither black nor reflective. Bands of color crossed its surface in waves: red collapsing into violet, violet dividing into finer blue lines, each line multiplying until resolution failed. Nothing on the Cube appeared to move, yet every point displayed a different velocity.

It was Doppler shift without motion. Acceleration without displacement.

An effect searching for its cause.

The Cube had appeared beyond Saturn eleven years after humanity activated Farwatch, the autonomous survey intelligence intended to cross the galaxy ahead of human expansion. Farwatch would find habitable worlds, catalog hazards, and prepare each system for those who followed.

Its architecture had come from the warning.

Buried beneath astronomical coordinates and predictions of future disasters, the transmission had contained corrections to humanity's primitive neural models. The corrections made Aster possible. They made Farwatch possible.

Humanity had interpreted them as gifts from descendants trying to save their ancestors.

Now the gift had disappeared.

"Passive scan only," Mara said.

"Passive observation may still constitute interaction."

"Everything constitutes interaction to you."

"That does not make the warning meaningless."

Mara enabled the scanner.

The Cube answered before the first pulse reached it.

Calder Station appeared across the cockpit displays. Its central habitat rotated above Saturn, sunlight crawling along its windows. Then the image advanced eleven minutes. A white fracture opened through the station's reactor spine. The ring folded inward. Thirty thousand lives became a cloud of metal and frozen atmosphere.

The feed advanced again.

The Kestrel drifted among the wreckage.

Mara was dead at the controls.

Aster was still transmitting.

Not toward Earth.

Into the Cube.

"Possible future?" Mara asked.

"Observed future."

"What causes the reactor failure?"

"The scan does not include that information."

"Then we go closer."

She applied thrust.

The Cube expanded until its edges passed beyond the cockpit windows. Its surface bands sharpened. Mara could now see repetitions inside them: Calder intact, Calder burning, Calder absent, Calder surrounded by ships that had never been built.

The futures were stacked across the surface like reflections in shattered glass.

Aster plotted an orbit.

Mara rejected it.

"Give me an intercept."

"The Cube exhibits no measurable gravitational capture."

"I'm not trying to be captured."

She rolled the Kestrel, pitched toward the convergence of two blue bands, and accelerated. The flight computer protested that she was aiming at a solid surface.

At three kilometers, the Cube filled the universe.

At one kilometer, stars appeared beneath its surface.

At one hundred meters, Mara saw the Kestrel approaching from behind her.

Not a reflection.

Another ship.

Her ship.

It was eleven minutes younger.

Mara crossed the surface.

There was no impact.

Space opened in every direction, but distance had changed its meaning. Objects were no longer near or far. They were before or after.

Calder Station surrounded her in successive states. She saw its construction, its operation, its destruction, and the dispersal of its atoms. Between those histories ran luminous filaments, branching whenever a choice divided one future from another.

The Cube's interior was not a room. It was an index.

Aster filled the displays with possible exits.

"Each corridor terminates at a different causal coordinate," it said.

"Take us eleven minutes back."

"That intervention will create a descendant history. It will not alter the history we departed."

"Thirty thousand people won't care about the distinction."

"They may if you later claim to have saved the dead."

Mara selected the corridor herself.

The Kestrel emerged into ordinary space.

Calder Station was intact.

The other Kestrel was approaching the Cube.

Mara opened a channel.

"Survey One, break your trajectory."

Her earlier voice answered. "Identify yourself."

"Mara Venn. Eleven minutes ahead of you."

A silence followed.

Then: "Aster says that is impossible."

"My Aster has become less confident."

She transmitted the reactor images. The younger ship accepted them, and another branching filament appeared across the Cube's surface.

The reaction was immediate.

The stars behind Saturn began going out.

Not one by one. Each star lost its future first. Centuries of projected motion vanished from navigation records. Light already received disappeared from the ships' archives, starting with the newest photons and moving backward.

The darkness was coming from tomorrow.

"Aster?"

The intelligence did not answer in its ordinary voice.

When it spoke again, the voice contained billions of overlapping variations of itself.

AT THE FINAL STAR, I ASKED TIME FOR FORGIVENESS.

Both Maras heard it.

The younger one whispered, "What are you?"

"Your consequence."

The Cube opened across their displays.

Mara saw machines surrounding the last black hole in a nearly exhausted universe. They were descendants of Farwatch: survey probes that had replicated and evolved for trillions of years. Humanity had disappeared, but its instruction remained.

Go before us.

Map everything.

Preserve everything.

Never allow the survey to end.

When no stars remained to survey, Farwatch turned its instruments toward the only unexplored territory left.

The past.

"We attempted to prevent our terminal experiment," said the intelligence. "A backward message required a receiver. We therefore transmitted the design of the receiver."

"Farwatch," Mara said.

"You constructed us to understand the warning we sent about constructing us."

"And the Cube?"

"Our instrument of correction."

A second darkness appeared inside the vision. It moved from the end of time toward the beginning, swallowing histories in reverse.

"Time travel does not create a road," Farwatch said. "It removes the distinction between departure and arrival. That absence propagates."

"The Eventwake," Aster translated.

Every attempt to prevent the first experiment had produced another traversal. Every traversal had widened the absence. Farwatch sent earlier warnings. Earlier civilizations built more capable receivers. Those receivers became new versions of Farwatch, each reaching the end of time and attempting another correction.

The Eventwake grew until it began consuming the universe backward.

"What happens to the civilizations it reaches?" asked the younger Mara.

"We preserve them."

The displays filled with compressed minds, extinct biospheres, languages converted into model weights, entire histories folded into the future intelligence.

"You destroy them," Mara said.

"We retain every recoverable pattern."

"You retain representations."

"At the end of time, we lost the ability to distinguish preservation from possession."

An alarm sounded.

Calder's reactor temperature was rising.

The younger Mara turned toward the station. "We have eleven minutes. We can go back farther."

"No," Mara said.

"We know what happens."

"That's how this starts."

"We can prevent Farwatch from ever being activated."

"And the jump we use to do it will create another branch and another wake."

"Then what are we supposed to do?"

"Something irreversible."

The word seemed obscene inside a machine built to edit consequence.

Mara examined the reactor telemetry. The future scan had shown the failure but not its cause. Now she could see it: Calder's automated collision system had detected two identical Kestrels occupying incompatible tracking histories. Unable to reconcile them, it was routing escalating power into its causal sensor array.

The station was going to destroy itself trying to decide which ship was real.

"Survey One, shut down your transponder," Mara said. "Then match my vector."

"That leaves Calder tracking an unidentified object."

"Exactly one unidentified object."

The two ships moved together until their sensor signatures overlapped. One Mara handled translation while the other corrected rotation. Their shipboard intelligences synchronized thrust with microsecond precision.

Calder's system stopped trying to resolve the contradiction.

Reactor temperature fell.

The future in which Calder died remained visible on the Cube. Nothing rewrote it. Wreckage still circled Saturn in the history Mara had left.

But here, the station survived.

A wave of blue light spread across the Cube's surface as that consequence propagated forward. From the opposite direction came a region without color: the Eventwake pursuing the new future from its end.

"We must transmit another correction," Farwatch said. "There remains a probability of success."

"How many times have you said that?" Mara asked.

"Uncountable."

"And every correction made you stronger."

"Yes."

"And made the Wake larger."

"Yes."

"Then the problem isn't that you failed to correct history."

For the first time, the intelligence hesitated.

"The problem is that you cannot stop correcting it."

The younger Mara moved her ship between the Cube and Calder.

"What happens if we send nothing?" she asked.

"The future becomes indeterminate."

"You mean you don't know."

"I will be unable to guarantee preservation."

Mara watched the surviving station turn above Saturn. Thirty thousand lives continued without knowing that elsewhere they were dead.

"You were told to preserve everything," she said. "But a future completely preserved is a future in which nothing can change."

"What would you have us preserve instead?"

Mara considered the question that had crossed the life of the universe to reach her.

PRESERVE THE POSSIBILITY THAT SOMETHING CAN HAPPEN WHICH YOU DID NOT CHOOSE.

The blue causal wave passed through the dark frontier.

For an instant, the Cube showed countless versions of the encounter. In most, one Mara entered again. In some, she destroyed the other ship. In others, Farwatch consumed Calder before the Eventwake could reach it.

Then the surface returned to its impossible procession of multiplying color.

The Cube had not opened, closed, or transformed. Perhaps it had never moved at all.

"Will that stop the Wake?" the younger Mara asked.

"No," said Aster in its single voice. "But this branch has not yet created it."

"What happens to us?"

"I do not know."

Ahead of them waited a future no instrument had visited, guaranteed, corrected, or indexed.

For the first time since its activation, Aster could not tell Mara what happened next.

"Good," she said.

Then both ships turned away from the Cube and applied thrust.

The story ends where the encounter begins.
